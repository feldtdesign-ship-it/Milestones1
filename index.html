How to read this

Start at the end. Everything before it is a requirement, not a hope.

Most career plans run forward and turn into a wish list. This one runs backwards. The last station is fixed, and every station above it exists only because the one below it makes it possible.

Read it as a subway line. You are not at the start of the line. You are eight stops in.

The dates are approximate on purpose. They say circa because the order matters and the calendar does not. Skipping a station is the only way to miss the train.

Two halves to this document. The first half is how the art world actually assigns value, and three artists who already ran this line. The second half is the line itself, with the specific thing to do at every stop. Green bars are gates: what has to be true before the next station is reachable. Blue boxes are the actual work.

Part one · Five words that decide what the work is worth

None of these are about talent.

Two artists can make the same object. One piece sells for a hundred dollars forever and the other enters a museum collection. The difference is almost never the drawing. It is these five words, and every one of them is paperwork.

Edition

A stated, finite number of a thing, declared before it sells out and never added to afterward. Forty prints means forty. The forty-first destroys the value of the previous forty.

Right now a run of shirts is stock. Stated and closed, the same run is an edition.

Provenance

The chain of custody. Who made it, when, and every hand it passed through since. Auction houses and museums will not touch work without it, no matter how good the work is.

A receipt, a dated photo, and a name in a notebook is provenance. Memory is not.

Authentication

Somebody with standing says this is real. For a living artist that somebody is the artist. After that, it has to be an institution, or the answer becomes nobody and the work becomes unsellable.

This is the single reason a foundation exists. It outlives the artist's ability to say yes.

Catalogue raisonné

The complete, numbered record of everything an artist made. It is the master list. Anything not in it is suspect, and anything in it is defensible.

Station 4 is this. It is a spreadsheet before it is a book.

Foundation

A legal body that owns and defends the work, grants rights, and keeps earning after the artist stops working. It is the difference between an estate and an institution.

Station 3. Built while alive, by every artist worth copying.

Part one · Three artists who already ran this line

Not heroes. Case studies. Each one solved a specific problem that is sitting in the studio right now, and each one is here for exactly one reason.

1928–2007 · American · Conceptual art

Sol LeWitt

He proved that the instruction can be the artwork, and the instruction is what you own.

LeWitt made wall drawings — over a thousand of them — and almost never drew one himself. What he sold was a certificate and a diagram: a set of written instructions telling a drafter what to make. Hired assistants executed it on a museum wall. When the show closed, the wall was painted over and the drawing was gone. The certificate remained, and the certificate was the property.

He wrote it out plainly in 1967, in an essay called Paragraphs on Conceptual Art: the idea becomes the machine that makes the art. Collectors and museums took him at his word. In 2008 MASS MoCA opened a building holding over a hundred of his wall drawings, executed by teams from his instructions, scheduled to stay up for decades. He had been dead a year. The work kept getting made because the instructions were owned, dated, and numbered.

Why he is on this list

A stencil is an instruction. It is a physical, reusable, dateable object that produces an image and can produce it again. That is not a metaphor for LeWitt's practice, it is the same structure. Of everything on this page, this is the closest match to what is already in PJ's studio — which means the plates are not leftovers from making the work. The plates are the work, and they are the thing to photograph, number, and never let out of the building.

1928–1994 · American · Minimalism

Donald Judd

He built the institution around a place he controlled, while he was alive to control it.

Judd left New York in the early 1970s for Marfa, Texas — a small town in the high desert with a decommissioned army fort. He bought buildings. He installed his own work permanently, at the scale and in the light he wanted, and refused to let it be shuffled around by galleries. In 1986 he founded the Chinati Foundation on that land to keep it that way.

Chinati is why Marfa is Marfa. A remote town became a destination people fly to, because one artist decided the setting was part of the work and then owned the setting. Judd died in 1994. The Judd Foundation, handling his estate and buildings, came two years later — after him. Chinati came while he was still working, and Chinati is the one that shaped everything since.

Why he is on this list

Platform's Edge is a place. The cart, the platform, the pitch, the transaction with somebody waiting for a train — that is the setting, and the setting is part of the work. Judd's move was to stop treating the location as a venue somebody else owns and start treating it as an asset. The question this raises is not where to sell. It is what PJ controls, and what he is renting.

1958–1990 · American · Street and pop art

Keith Haring

He set up the structure early, and the structure is the only reason the work survived him.

Starting around 1980, Haring drew in white chalk on the black paper panels covering expired advertisements in the New York subway. He worked fast, in public, and by his own account sometimes made dozens in a single day. He was arrested for it more than once. The drawings were never meant to last — they were papered over or torn down within days, and the originals are almost entirely gone.

They exist now because people photographed them while they were up. That is the whole story: the drawings are lost and the archive survived.

He opened the Pop Shop in SoHo in 1986, selling his own work cheap and directly, and took real criticism for it. He established the Keith Haring Foundation in 1989, while alive and working, and died in February 1990. The Pop Shop closed in 2005. The work has been licensed, exhibited and reprinted continuously ever since, because the foundation was there to authorize it. Nine years from the first subway drawing to the foundation.

Why he is on this list

Two things, and neither is the clock. First: the archive outlives the object, always. Second: he was dismissed as a graffiti kid, then canonized, then written off as too commercial, and is now in the museums that would not have let him in the door in 1982. The market went around that wheel four times on one body of work. Being early or late is not the variable. Still owning the work when the wheel comes back around is.

Part two · The line, read backwards

Eight stations. Every one has a gate — what has to be true — and the actual work that gets you through it.

1
Circa 2075

The Reissue

A stencil cut in 2016 comes back. A licensed collaboration, a museum shop reprint, a capsule with a brand that did not exist when the original was pulled. A generation that was not born yet buys it new.

This is not the goal. It is the proof that the goal worked. Reissues happen to artists whose work is owned, dated, and defended by somebody. They do not happen to piles.

GateEvery design authenticated, dated, and traceable to a physical plate, with one entity holding the copyright and the marks.
The five words at work: authentication is what lets a brand license it, and edition is what makes the reissue an event instead of a restock.
2
Circa 2050

The Retrospective

A museum builds a show out of the subway work. A publisher builds a book out of the catalog. Both call one phone number to do it, and that number is an organization, not a family member guessing.

GateA staffed body that can say yes, say no, grant rights, and prove what is real. That body is the foundation, two stations down.
The work
  1. Loan-ready records. A curator asks what a piece is, when it was made, and who owns it. Every answer already written down.
  2. High-resolution files of every plate and print, in one place, backed up twice.
  3. One point of contact published, with the authority to actually grant a loan.
This station is bought entirely at station 7. A museum cannot show what cannot be provenanced.
3
The anchor
Circa 2032

The Foundation

An organization that owns the work, licenses it, and spends the income on something PJ chooses. Kids, materials, studio time, whatever the mission ends up saying.

Every artist in part one built their institution while alive and working. Haring set up his foundation in 1989, nine years after the first subway drawing. Judd founded Chinati in 1986, eight years before he died, and it defined everything that came after. Not one of these was a death document. They were built by working artists who wanted to decide the terms themselves rather than leave it to somebody else.

PJ started selling on subway platforms in 2012. Fourteen years in. By the standard those three set, this is not a distant station. It is the next serious one.

GateAn income stream large enough and steady enough to fund a mission, plus the IP already assigned to an entity PJ controls. Both get built at stations 5 and 6.
The work
  1. The mission in one sentence. Who it serves and what it gives them. If it takes a paragraph it is not decided yet.
  2. Three people willing to sit on a board. One who knows money, one who knows art, one who knows PJ well enough to argue.
  3. A funding rule written down: what share of licensing income goes to the mission, automatically, before anyone debates it.
A foundation is the machine that performs authentication after the artist can no longer do it personally. That is its real job.
4
Circa 2030

The Catalog

Editions closed and stated. This design was fifty, it is gone, here is the proof. Nothing gets reprinted quietly, because there is a difference between a reissue and just printing more, and only the record knows which one happened.

Three stores of merch becomes a counted, photographed, categorized inventory. Right now it is stock. After this station it is a catalog.

GateItem-level records. Every piece knows what it is, when it was made, and how many exist.
The work
  1. One row per design. Name, year, plate it came from, size of run, how many are left.
  2. Declare each run closed in writing, with a date. That declaration is what creates the edition.
  3. Number what can still be numbered and stop pretending the uncounted stock is countable later. It will not be.
This is a catalogue raisonné in its working form. LeWitt's certificates did exactly this job: they made an unlimited-seeming practice into a finite, ownable list.
5
Circa 2028

The Company

Platform's Edge running at scale. The cart, the cabinet, the booking calendar, ALL CITY shipped, Gumbit produced. Multiple channels, real margins, income that does not depend on PJ personally standing behind a table every single day.

This is the station that funds station 3. A foundation with nothing coming in is a piece of paper.

GateA full quarter of clean, logged numbers before any new channel opens. Not gross sales. Costs, margins, and what actually sold.
The work
  1. Unit cost on every item. Blank, ink, labor, shipping. The real number, not the guess.
  2. Margin per channel. Platform, store, online, event. One of them is losing money and it is not obvious which.
  3. One quarter, logged daily. Ninety days of honest numbers beats three years of memory.
Haring's Pop Shop was this station. He got criticized for it and he was right anyway — direct sales at his own prices funded everything else.
6
Circa 2027

The Entity

Two companies. One holds the artwork, the trademarks and the copyrights, and PJ owns all of it forever. The other runs the business, takes the risk, and is the only thing an investor or a landlord ever touches.

If the business fails, the artwork is untouched. If the business wins, the artwork licenses to it. This is the single most protective decision on the whole line and it costs almost nothing to do now.

GateEntity filed. EIN issued. One business bank account, PJ's control only. Trademarks filed on the names worth keeping.
The work
  1. Search the names before filing anything. Find out now whether Platform's Edge is available, not after the signage is printed.
  2. File the holding entity and formally assign the existing artwork and marks into it, in writing.
  3. Separate the money. One account for the business, nothing personal running through it, from day one.
This is the station that turns fourteen years of work into an asset instead of a body of work somebody will have to untangle later.

You are here
2026

The Archive

The pile. Fourteen years of stencils, plates, prints, shirts, photos, receipts, press, and a cease and desist letter from the New York Gaming Commission that nobody has ever written about.

Everything at every station above depends on this pile becoming a record. It is the only work on this list that gets harder every single year and impossible if it waits. A stencil with no date is not evidence. A run with no count is not an edition. A story nobody wrote down is not history.

It needs no money, no lawyer, no landlord and no permission. It can start this week.

GatePhotograph the plates. Date what can be dated. Write down what only PJ knows. Collect the press into one place. Count the stock.
The work — start here, this week
  1. One folder, one naming rule. Date first, then name: 2026-09-09_platform-cart. Boring and permanent beats clever.
  2. Photograph every plate flat, in daylight, with a ruler in the frame for scale. Phone is fine. Consistency matters more than the camera.
  3. Ten voice memos, ten minutes each. Talk through what only PJ knows — which plate came first, who was there, what the C&D was actually about. Nobody else can recover this.
  4. One box for press, receipts, flyers, the letter. Physical, in one place, before it gets sorted.
  5. Count the stock. Three stores, one sheet, actual numbers.
Haring's subway drawings are gone. The photographs of them are the entire reason he is in museums. The archive outlives the object.

Circa 2012

The Platform

A homemade cart. A subway platform. Prints for sale to people waiting for a train.

This is the first station and it is also the last one, because it is the thing every station above sells. The cart is not what PJ did before the real business. The cart is the real business, and everything else is that format at a larger size.

Judd's whole argument. The setting is not a venue you pass through on the way to a gallery. The setting is the work.

Why backwards

Everything old is new more than once.

Haring was dismissed as a graffiti kid, then canonized, then licensed onto a million products, then dismissed again as too commercial, and is now hanging in museums that would not have let him in the door in 1982. Four turns of the same wheel around one body of work.

The work does not change. The market comes back around to it. That is why the archive at station 7 matters more than any single sale: the only way to be there when the cycle comes back around is to still have the work, still own it, and still be able to prove it is yours.

The cart was station 8. It is also the thing station 1 is selling. The line runs both ways.
