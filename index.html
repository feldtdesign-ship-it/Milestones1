A map of PJ O'Rourke II's career, read from the last stop backwards to the platform he is standing on.

Draft v3  ·  For PJ  ·  Feldt Design
This is a Reissue-bound train. The next stop is the Archive. Stand clear.

How to read this

Start at the end. Work back to where you're standing.

Most plans run forward and turn into a wish list. This one runs backwards. The last stop is fixed, and every stop above it is there because the one below it makes it possible.

Read it as a subway line, because it is one. Fourteen years in, PJ is not at the start of the line. He is most of the way down it.

E

The ExpressFive stops. The ones that change what PJ legally owns and what anyone can prove. Big dot on the map.

L

The LocalAll eight. Same track, same terminal, three extra stops where the money and the record get built. Small dot on the map.

Both trains arriveThe Express gets to the Foundation sooner. The Local gets there with the catalog counted, the numbers clean, and the show already booked. Neither one is wrong. Know which one you're on.

On every platform

A quote from someone who already rode this line. Between the stops, what to do while the train is moving. Dates say circa on purpose — the order matters, the calendar doesn't.

The line, read backwards

STOP 1 Circa 2075 Express · Terminal

The Reissue

What this is

A stencil cut in 2016 comes back. A licensed collaboration. A museum shop reprint. A capsule with a brand that didn't exist when the original sold out. People who weren't born yet buy it new and think it's new.

What to look at

This isn't the goal. It's the receipt that says the goal worked. Reissues happen to artists whose work is owned, dated and defended by somebody. They don't happen to piles.

What has to be true

Every design authenticated, dated and traceable to a physical plate, with one entity holding the copyright and the marks. All of that gets built further down the line.

Art is for everybody.

Keith Haring
On the train  ·  Stop 2 to Stop 1
Let it come back around

This is the longest ride on the line and there is almost nothing to do on it. The work is made. The record is kept. The market goes away and comes back on its own schedule, and it always comes back. The only job here is to still own the thing when it does.

STOP 2 Circa 2050 Local only

The Retrospective

What this is

A museum builds a show out of the subway work. A publisher builds a book out of the catalog. Both call one phone number to do it, and that number is an organization.

What to look at

The Express skips this stop, and it should — you can't schedule a museum show. It happens because the records are good enough that saying yes is easy. Curators go where the work is already documented. Every time.

What to have ready

  • Loan-ready records. A curator asks what a piece is, when it was made and who owns it. Every answer already written down.
  • High-resolution files of every plate and print, one place, backed up twice.
  • One contact with the authority to actually grant a loan.

The photographs are why the drawings still exist.

Martha Cooper · on shooting the trains in the seventies
On the train  ·  Stop 3 to Stop 2
Answer the phone

Once the foundation exists, people start asking. A student writing a thesis. A blog. A small gallery upstate. Say yes to the small ones and keep a file of every ask. That file is what a museum reads before it calls.

STOP 3 Circa 2032 Express · Major

The Foundation

What this is

An organization that owns the work, licenses it, and spends the income on something PJ picks. Kids, materials, studio time, a cart for somebody else. Whatever the mission ends up saying.

What to look at

Every artist worth copying built this while they were working, not at the end. Haring set his up in 1989 with a shop running and a full studio. Judd founded Chinati in 1986 and spent the next eight years using it. These were not exit plans. They were working artists deciding they'd rather set the terms themselves than let somebody else do it later.

PJ has been on the platform since 2012. Fourteen years of work is not a warm-up for this. It's the qualification.

What to have ready

  • The mission in one sentence. Who it serves and what they get. If it takes a paragraph it isn't decided yet.
  • Three people for a board. One who knows money, one who knows art, one who knows PJ well enough to argue with him.
  • A funding rule in writing. What share of licensing income goes to the mission, automatically, before anybody debates it.

It takes a great deal of time and thought to install work properly.

Donald Judd · who bought the buildings instead of renting the walls
On the train  ·  Stop 4 to Stop 3
Decide who it's for

This ride is a thinking ride, not a paperwork ride. Name the mission out loud to five different people and watch which version makes them lean in. That's the sentence. Write it down the same day you hear it.

STOP 4 Circa 2030 Local only

The Catalog

What this is

Editions closed and stated. This design was fifty, it's gone, here's the proof. Three stores of merch becomes a counted, photographed, categorized inventory. Right now it's stock. After this stop it's a catalog.

What to look at

A closed edition is what makes a reissue an event instead of a restock. Same object, completely different value, and the only thing separating them is a number somebody wrote down and stuck to.

What to have ready

  • One row per design. Name, year, plate it came from, size of run, how many left.
  • Declare each run closed in writing, with a date. That declaration is what creates the edition.
  • Number what can still be numbered. Start with what's on the shelf today.

The idea becomes a machine that makes the art.

Sol LeWitt · 1967 · he sold the instruction, never the wall
On the train  ·  Stop 5 to Stop 4
Count while you sell

Don't stop selling to build the catalog. Build it out of the selling. Every time something goes out the door it gets a line: what it was, what run it came from, what's left. Ten seconds a sale, and by the time you pull into Stop 4 the work is already done.

STOP 5 Circa 2028 Local only

The Company

What this is

Platform's Edge running at scale. The cart, the cabinet, the booking calendar, ALL CITY shipped, Gumbit produced. Multiple channels, real margins, money coming in that doesn't require PJ to personally stand behind a table every single day.

What to look at

This is the stop that pays for Stop 3. A foundation with nothing coming in is a piece of paper in a drawer. Haring took real heat for opening a shop and selling his own work cheap, and he was right — the shop funded everything else and put the work in more hands than any gallery ever did.

What to have ready

  • Unit cost on every item. Blank, ink, labor, shipping. The real number.
  • Margin per channel. Platform, store, online, event. One of them is quietly losing money and it isn't the obvious one.
  • Ninety days logged daily. One honest quarter beats three years of memory.

Being good in business is the most fascinating kind of art.

Andy Warhol
On the train  ·  Stop 6 to Stop 5
Run one channel all the way clean

Not all four at once. Pick the strongest one, get its numbers honest end to end, and use it as the template for the rest. A channel you understand completely beats four you're guessing at.

STOP 6 Circa 2027 Express · Major

The Entity

What this is

Two companies. One holds the artwork, the trademarks and the copyrights, and PJ owns all of it forever. The other one runs the business, takes the risk, and is the only thing an investor or a landlord ever touches.

What to look at

If the business has a bad year, the artwork isn't in the room. If the business has a great year, the artwork licenses to it and gets paid. This is the cheapest protective move on the entire line and it's available right now.

What to have ready

  • Search the names first. Find out what's available before the signage gets printed, not after.
  • File the holding entity and formally assign the existing artwork and marks into it, in writing.
  • One business bank account. Nothing personal running through it, starting day one.

Own the name before somebody sells it back to you.

Every artist who learned it the hard way
On the train  ·  Stop 7 to Stop 6
Make the list of what's worth keeping

Short ride. While the archive is still fresh, write down every name, mark and design that PJ would be angry to lose. That list is exactly what gets filed at Stop 6, and it's much easier to write with the plates still spread out on the table.

STOP 7 2026 · You are here Express · Major

The Archive

Now arriving. This stop needs no money, no lawyer, no landlord and nobody's permission. It can start this week.

What this is

The pile. Fourteen years of stencils, plates, prints, shirts, photos, receipts, press, and a cease and desist letter from the New York Gaming Commission that nobody has ever written about.

What to look at

Every stop above this one is built out of this pile. A stencil with a date is evidence. A run with a count is an edition. A story written down is history. Same objects either way — the only difference is whether somebody wrote it down.

And the good news is that the hard part is finished. Fourteen years of making the work is the part most people never do. This is just the part where it gets a name and a number.

Start this week

  • One folder, one naming rule. Date first, then name. Boring and permanent beats clever.
  • Photograph every plate flat, daylight, ruler in the frame for scale. A phone is fine. Consistency matters more than the camera.
  • Ten voice memos, ten minutes each. Which plate came first, who was there, what the C&D was really about. Nobody else on earth can recover this.
  • One box for press, receipts, flyers, the letter.
  • Count the stock. Three stores, one sheet, real numbers.

Just DO!

Sol LeWitt · 1965 · in a letter to a friend who was stuck
On the train  ·  Stop 8 to Stop 7
Keep the cart running

This is the ride PJ has already been on for fourteen years, and it isn't over. Nothing about the archive means stepping off the platform. The cart keeps going out. The difference from here on is that what goes out gets written down on the way.

STOP 8 Circa 2012 Express · Origin

The Platform

What this is

A homemade cart. A subway platform. Prints for sale to people waiting for a train.

What to look at

First stop on the line and also the last one, because it's the thing every stop above it sells. The cart is not what PJ did before the real business started. The cart is the real business. Everything after this is the same move at a bigger size.

I don't think about art when I'm working. I try to think about life.

Jean-Michel Basquiat

Why backwards

Everything old is new more than once.

Haring got called a graffiti kid, then got canonized, then got written off as too commercial, and now hangs in museums that wouldn't have let him in the door in 1982. Four turns of the same wheel around one body of work, and the work never changed once.

The market leaves and the market comes back. That's not a risk, it's a schedule. The only thing that decides whether PJ is there when it comes back around is whether he still has the work, still owns it, and can still prove it's his.

The cart was Stop 8. The cart is also what Stop 1 is selling. The line runs both ways.

This is the last stop on this train. Everybody stays on.
